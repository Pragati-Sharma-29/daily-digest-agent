## Security Vulnerabilities & AI Risk

### What happened
Today featured a cluster of significant security stories spanning AI systems, hardware, and critical event infrastructure. A critical Microsoft Copilot vulnerability enabled 2FA theft, AMD quietly removed memory encryption from consumer chips, and a FIFA World Cup broadcast system was found to be wide open to external manipulation.

### Key stories
- **Critical Microsoft Copilot "SearchLeak" vulnerability enabled 2FA code theft** — Researchers discovered a critical exploit in Microsoft Copilot that allowed attackers to steal two-factor authentication codes from users, using a technique that highlights fundamental weaknesses in how the industry approaches LLM security. Ars Technica notes this is a recurring failure pattern — LLMs introduce new attack surfaces that traditional security models are not designed to handle. The vulnerability underscores that integrating AI deeply into productivity tools creates enterprise security risks that are still not well understood. ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/06/critical-copilot-vulnerability-allowed-hackers-to-seal-2fa-code-from-users/))
- **AMD stripped memory encryption from consumer CPUs without disclosure** — AMD removed Transparent Secure Memory Encryption (TSME) from its consumer processor lineup in what security researchers are characterizing as a deliberate and covert decision made without user notification. This is particularly concerning because TSME provides protection against cold boot attacks and physical memory extraction. Users and security professionals are calling foul, and the incident raises serious questions about AMD's security transparency practices. ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/06/users-cry-foul-after-amd-stripped-memory-crypto-from-its-consumer-cpus/))
- **FIFA World Cup system bug gave anyone control of global TV broadcast stream** — A security researcher discovered that a vulnerability in FIFA's internal online platforms would have allowed any unauthorized user to access and modify the live TV stream broadcast for every World Cup match — a stunning failure given the scale of the event and its global audience. The bug demonstrates how high-profile sporting events are expanding attack surfaces by centralizing broadcast control in internet-connected systems. FIFA has not yet disclosed the full scope of access the researcher obtained. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/16/bug-in-fifa-world-cup-internal-system-gave-anyone-ability-to-modify-tv-stream/))
- **Google Cloud introduces AI Threat Defense and new CISO** — Google Cloud announced Chris Betz as its new CISO and published four key lessons that shaped the development of its AI Threat Defense product, signaling that Google sees AI-powered security operations as a major competitive differentiator in the cloud market. The company was also named a Leader in the IDC MarketScape SIEM 2026 assessment. ([Google Cloud Blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/identity-security/cloud-ciso-perspectives-the-4-lessons-that-guided-ai-threat-defense/))

### What to watch
Watch for regulatory responses to the AMD TSME removal — European data protection authorities in particular may take a dim view of undisclosed security capability reductions. Also track whether the FIFA breach results in coordinated disclosure and what changes event organizers make to broadcast infrastructure security.

## AI Infrastructure & Data Centers

### What happened
The physical infrastructure challenge of AI continued to generate major announcements today. Google Cloud unveiled a new liquid cooling retrofit solution for existing data centers, and MIT Technology Review published analysis on how "demand flexibility" programs can get AI data centers online faster by working with the electrical grid rather than waiting for capacity expansion.

### Key stories
- **Google introduces "Brazos" liquid cooling for air-cooled data centers** — Google Cloud's Brazos system is designed to retrofit liquid cooling capabilities into existing air-cooled data centers — a critical innovation given that next-gen AI chips regularly exceed 1,000W thermal design power, far beyond what air cooling can handle. Rather than requiring completely new facility builds, Brazos enables operators to upgrade existing infrastructure. This has major implications for the speed at which the industry can expand AI compute capacity without starting from scratch. ([Google Cloud Blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/systems/brazos-liquid-cooling-system-for-air-cooled-data-centers/))
- **"Demand flexibility" emerges as a strategy to accelerate data center grid connections** — MIT Technology Review examined how data centers can get online faster by adopting flexible demand programs — agreeing to curtail power usage during grid stress events in exchange for faster interconnection approvals. The piece uses the UK grid as a case study, noting how sudden demand spikes (like a World Cup halftime tea break) illustrate the challenge of adding massive new constant loads. This is an underappreciated constraint on AI infrastructure expansion timelines. ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/06/16/1138591/data-center-online-quickly-electric-grid-flex/))
- **DOJ argues xAI's unpermitted gas turbines are a "national security" matter** — The Justice Department sided with xAI, Elon Musk's AI company, arguing that the Pentagon's need for xAI's computing capacity justifies allowing the company to continue operating unpermitted natural gas turbines. The intervention is extraordinary — it effectively subordinates environmental permitting law to national security arguments around AI compute access. This sets a troubling precedent for how AI infrastructure buildout may bypass normal regulatory processes. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/16/doj-claims-xais-unpermitted-gas-turbines-are-a-matter-of-national-economic-and-energy-security/))

### What to watch
Track regulatory responses to the xAI/DOJ gas turbine situation — state environmental agencies may push back hard against federal preemption arguments. Also watch whether Brazos-style liquid cooling retrofits become an industry standard solution or whether Google keeps it proprietary as a competitive advantage.

This matters because local model viability reduces dependence on cloud AI providers, has significant privacy implications for enterprise and individual users, and could democratize AI access in bandwidth-constrained or regulated environments. ([Vicki Boykis via Hacker News](https://vickiboykis.com/2026/06/15/running-local-models-is-good-now/))
